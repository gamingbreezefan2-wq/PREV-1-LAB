2

While using a microscope you switched from the 4X objective to the 40X objective. What happens to the following? (explain briefly)

Lab 2 slides

a- Working distance: decreases. Working distance is the distance between the objective lens and the specimen. The 40× objective is longer and sits much closer to the slide, so you focus with the fine adjustment knob only.

b- Field of view: decreases. At higher magnification you see a smaller area of the specimen (about 1/10 of the diameter when going from 4× to 40×). The image also becomes dimmer, so you open the iris diaphragm.

c- Depth of vision (depth of field): decreases. Fewer layers of the specimen are in focus at the same time. In the colored-threads exercise, all three threads were in focus at low power, but not at high power. Low power gives the greatest depth of field.

3

Why do we use the following while using a microscope?

Part a beyond slides

a- Oil immersion is used with the 100× objective. Immersion oil has about the same refractive index as glass. Light passing from the slide into the lens is therefore not bent (refracted) and scattered by an air gap. More light enters the objective, which gives better resolution and a brighter, sharper image at the highest magnification.

Your slides only list “oil immersion (100×)”. The explanation above is the standard textbook answer.

b- Staining increases contrast, which makes thin, nearly transparent specimens and their structures visible (Lab 2: dyes “increase contrast and make the specimen more visible”). Examples from your labs: methylene blue on onion cells, iodine to show the nucleus and stain starch (amyloplasts), and neutral red on red-onion cells.

4

Which microscope has a better resolution, the light or the electron microscope? Why?

Lab 2 + textbook

The electron microscope. It uses a beam of electrons instead of visible light. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, and resolution (the ability to tell two close points apart as separate) is limited by wavelength. An electron microscope can therefore separate points far closer together. A light microscope can resolve down to about 0.1 µm at best, while electron microscopes reach the nanometer range.

From your slides: the TEM gives the highest magnification of all microscopes. The trade-off is that specimens must be dead: thin-sectioned and metal-stained for TEM, or metal-coated surfaces for SEM (3D images).

5

Name one advantage and 2 disadvantages of the inverted light microscope.

Beyond slides

In an inverted microscope the light source and condenser are above the stage, and the objectives are below it, looking up.

Advantage: you can view living cells in culture flasks or Petri dishes from underneath, because the cells settle on the bottom. You don't need a coverslip and you don't disturb the cells, and large containers fit on the stage.

Disadvantages:

  1. Lower resolution and magnification. You look through the thick bottom of the flask or dish, so it needs long-working-distance objectives, and oil immersion is usually not practical.
  2. More expensive and bulkier than a standard upright compound microscope.

The inverted microscope isn't in your Lab 2 slides. This is the standard textbook answer.

2

Name three types of polysaccharides and explain the function of each.

Lab 1 + Lab 3
  1. Starch stores energy (glucose) in plants. It is kept in amyloplasts, especially in roots and tubers such as the potato, and turns bluish-black with iodine.
  2. Glycogen stores energy (glucose) in animals, mainly in the liver and muscles.
  3. Cellulose is structural. It is the main component of plant cell walls and gives the cell support and rigidity.

Also accepted: chitin, which is structural and forms arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls.

3

Draw two amino acids linked by a peptide bond.

Lab 1 slides

The peptide bond forms by dehydration (condensation) synthesis. The –OH from the carboxyl group of amino acid 1 and an –H from the amino group of amino acid 2 leave as H2O. The C of the carboxyl group then bonds to the N, giving the C–N peptide bond.

Amino acid 1 + amino acid 2 → dipeptide + H2O

This C–N bond is what the Biuret test detects (Cu2+ complexes with peptide bonds → violet). Free amino acids have no peptide bonds, so they test negative.

4

Why does the body store energy in the form of lipids and not in any other form of organic compounds?

Lab 1 + textbook
  • More energy per gram: lipids supply more than twice as many calories per gram as carbohydrates or proteins (Lab 1), roughly 9 vs. 4 kcal/g. This is because they are rich in energy-rich C–H bonds.
  • Compact, light storage: lipids are hydrophobic, so they are stored without water. Glycogen and starch hold a lot of water, so storing the same energy as carbohydrate would make the body much heavier.

Stored fat also insulates and cushions organs.

5

What is the difference between steroids and other lipids? What role do these compounds play in the body?

Lab 1 + textbook

Difference: steroids have a carbon skeleton of four fused rings and contain no fatty acids. Other lipids are built from fatty-acid chains: fats (triglycerides) are glycerol + 3 fatty acids, phospholipids are glycerol + 2 fatty acids + a phosphate group, and waxes are fatty acids + long-chain alcohols. Steroids are still classed as lipids because they are hydrophobic.

Roles of steroids:

  • Cholesterol is a component of animal cell membranes (it regulates fluidity) and the precursor of the other steroids.
  • Steroid hormones such as testosterone, estrogen and cortisol act as chemical signals.

Other lipids: fats store energy, insulate and cushion; phospholipids form the membrane bilayer; waxes make a waterproof coating.

III. Cell Structure and Function

1

What component causes the structural difference between the smooth endoplasmic reticulum and the rough endoplasmic reticulum? What additional role does this component give to the ER?

Lab 3 + textbook

Ribosomes. The rough ER is studded with ribosomes on its outer (cytoplasmic) surface, which gives it the “rough” look. The smooth ER has no ribosomes.

Added role: protein synthesis. Ribosomes are the organelles of protein synthesis (Lab 3). The rough ER therefore makes proteins, mainly those to be secreted, built into membranes, or sent to lysosomes. These proteins enter the ER to be folded and processed before going to the Golgi. The smooth ER's own roles are lipid synthesis and detoxification.

2

What is the function of the mitochondrion? Is this organelle also present in plant cells? How would you explain that, knowing that plant cells obtain ATP by photosynthesis?

Lab 3 slides

Function: the mitochondrion is the site of aerobic (cellular) respiration. It breaks down sugars using O2 to produce ATP. Its inner membrane folds into cristae, which hold the respiratory enzymes in place.

Yes, plant cells have mitochondria. Photosynthesis in the chloroplast uses light energy to make sugar. The ATP made in the light reactions is used inside the chloroplast to build that sugar, not to power the rest of the cell. To get ATP for its own activities, the plant cell must break down that sugar (or stored starch) in its mitochondria. This happens at night too, and in non-green cells with no chloroplasts, such as roots, potato tubers and onion bulbs.

3

Draw meiosis for a cell having 2n = 4 chromosomes.

Lab 4 slides
maternal homolog paternal homolog centromere recombinant chromatid (after crossing-over)

What your drawing must show

  • 2 homologous pairs (one long, one short pair): 2n = 4. Each chromosome is replicated into 2 sister chromatids before meiosis.
  • Prophase I: homologs synapse into bivalents, and crossing-over happens at chiasmata.
  • Metaphase I: pairs line up at the plate. Anaphase I: homologs separate while sister chromatids stay together.
  • Telophase I: 2 cells with n = 2 (still 2 chromatids each). This is the reduction division.
  • Meiosis II works like mitosis: chromosomes line up singly, and sister chromatids separate in anaphase II.
  • Result: 4 haploid cells (n = 2), genetically different from each other and from the parent cell.
4

What is the function of the following organelles?

Lab 3 + textbook

a- Golgi body: modifies, sorts and packages proteins (and lipids) received from the ER into vesicles. It sends them to the plasma membrane for secretion or to other organelles, and it forms lysosomes.

b- Lysosome: a membrane sac of hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes. It handles intracellular digestion: it breaks down macromolecules, engulfed food particles or bacteria, and worn-out organelles.

c- Vacuole: in a mature plant cell, the large central vacuole fills about 90% of the cell's volume and is bounded by the vacuolar membrane. It stores water, ions, organic and inorganic molecules, enzymes and waste products (Lab 3). It also keeps the cell turgid by pushing the cytoplasm against the cell wall, which is what is lost in plasmolysis.

d- Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the plasma membrane, made mainly of cellulose in plants. It gives support, shape and protection and stops the cell from bursting in a hypotonic solution. The middle lamella holds adjacent cell walls together, and plasmodesmata pass through the walls to connect neighboring cells (Lab 3).

The vacuole and cell wall are covered in Lab 3. The Golgi and lysosome are named but not explained there, so those two use the standard textbook definitions.

IV. Diffusion and Osmosis

1

A gardener was watering his plants regularly from a new water source, but he noticed that the plants were wilting (losing strength). What would you predict the salinity of the water to be relative to that of the plant (hypertonic or hypotonic)?

Lab 5 worksheet

Hypertonic. The water has a higher salt (solute) concentration than the plant cells. By osmosis, water leaves the cells, moving from the higher water concentration inside to the lower water concentration outside. The cells lose turgor pressure (the cytoplasm shrinks away from the wall, which is plasmolysis), so the plant wilts.

Same idea as your lab: onion cells plasmolyzed in 30% NaCl, and potato cylinders lost mass in the 30% (hypertonic) solution.

2

You have three unlabeled solutions: 1M Methylene Blue (MW 374 g/mol), 1M Coomassie Blue (MW 825.97 g/mol) and 1M Eosin (MW 624.05 g/mol). Eosin is red, but the other two are both blue. What experiment can you perform, knowing the molecular weight of each, to distinguish between the two blue solutions?

Lab 5 worksheet

Diffusion in agar (like Procedure 9.2).

  1. Place an equal-sized drop of each blue solution on the same agar plate. Use the same volume and the same 1 M concentration, at the same temperature.
  2. Wait the same amount of time, then measure the diameter of each colored halo with a ruler.

Interpretation: diffusion rate is inversely related to molecular weight. The smaller molecule diffuses faster and makes the larger halo.

  • Larger halo → Methylene blue (374 g/mol)
  • Smaller halo → Coomassie blue (825.97 g/mol)

Check: if you add eosin (624.05 g/mol) to the plate, its red halo should be in between. In your lab, KMnO4 (158) gave the largest halo, methylene blue (374) a middle one, and malachite green (929) the smallest.

3

You prepared two solutions (solution 1: 10% NaCl; solution 2: 50% NaCl) but forgot to label them. Using a dialysis bag, clips, and a beaker, how do you proceed to distinguish the two solutions?

Lab 5 worksheet

Osmosis across the dialysis membrane (like Procedure 9.4).

  1. Fill the dialysis bag with one of the solutions (call it A) and close both ends with the clips. Rinse, blot dry and weigh it (initial mass).
  2. Fill the beaker with the other solution (B) and submerge the bag in it.
  3. After about 30 min, remove the bag, blot it dry and weigh it again. You can also simply look at whether it swelled or shrank.
  • Bag gained mass (swelled): water moved in, so the bag's content is hypertonic. The bag holds 50% NaCl and the beaker 10%.
  • Bag lost mass (shrank): the bag's content is hypotonic. The bag holds 10% NaCl and the beaker 50%.

Water always moves by osmosis toward the side with the higher solute concentration. Na+ and Cl− are small enough to slowly cross dialysis tubing (Na+ and OH− crossed in your Exercise 2). So read the result after a fixed time, as in lab, rather than waiting until equilibrium.
